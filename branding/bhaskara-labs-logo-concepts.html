“Bhāskara” — the light-maker; honoring Bhāskarāchārya, who five centuries after Varahamihira held the same chair at the Ujjain observatory. Instantaneous motion before Newton, the chakravala before Fermat, the Līlāvatī before anyone thought arithmetic could be tender. Same rule as the sibling marks mihir labs and meluha labs: a quiet geometric symbol + a confident lowercase wordmark on the modern-India palette — with deep indigo as Bhaskara’s own signature: the dusk sky every calculation was checked against.

Chakravala — the cyclic algorithm

Bhāskara perfected the chakravala — the cyclic method — an iterative algorithm that cracked x² − 61y² = 1 five centuries before Fermat posed the same case to Europe as an impossible challenge. Hankel called it “the finest thing achieved in the theory of numbers before Lagrange.” The mark draws one run of the loop: eight steps circling inward, each landing heavier than the last, the final step set in indigo — and the iteration converging on the dusk-gradient solution. The most “labs” of the three: a logo that is literally an algorithm converging.

Jyā — the bowstring that named the sine

Every sine in every textbook is a loan-word from this desk. Indian astronomers measured the half-chord of an arc — jyā, “bowstring” — and Bhāskara I compressed its values into one famous approximation. Carried into Arabic as jība, misread as jayb, rendered into Latin as sinus: the sine. The mark restores the original picture — bow, string, and arrow (the versine really was called śara, “the arrow”), the named half-chord lit in gradient, the right-angle foot set in indigo, the arrowhead piercing the bow the way the patha mast threads its star road. Quietly the most subversive: the world’s trigonometry, signed by its owner.

Līlāvatī — the broken necklace

Bhāskara named his masterpiece for his daughter and taught arithmetic through her world — swans on lakes, swarms of bees, and a pearl necklace that snapped mid-dance: a third to the floor, a fifth to the bed… six pearls stayed on the string. The mark hangs those six on their catenary — indigo clasp at centre — while the seventh, the unknown, falls in gradient toward the floor line. Mathematics as something loved rather than feared; the warmest of the three and the natural landing-page hero.

The family

One grammar, one day

Three sibling marks built from the same parts — ink structure, one gradient life, indigo decision points: mihir watches the sun rise (the fixed point), meluha ships by the stars (the route), bhaskara closes the ledger at dusk (the proof). Observatory, harbour, desk — and the gradients run dawn, kiln, dusk: a full working day.

Recommendation: 01 Chakravala as the primary brand mark — for a lab there is no better story than an eight-hundred-year-old algorithm, and the converging spiral keeps the family’s circular geometry while being unmistakably about iteration, not the sun. Use 02 Jyā as the app icon / favicon, where bow-string-arrow stays legible at 16–48 px, and 03 Līlāvatī as the landing-page hero illustration rather than the logo. Wordmark set in Clash Display SemiBold, all-lowercase “bhaskara” with “labs” in regular; the Devanagari भास्कर sits as a secondary line on stationery and the footer, never inside the mark. One footnote that argues the name has range: when India launched its first earth-observation satellite in 1979, it was named Bhāskara — the name already ships instruments.
