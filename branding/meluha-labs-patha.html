patha — the star route

Cargo below, constellation above: the arc of stars is the road. Developed here with the same dimensional play as the mihir labs Graha sun — and rooted one layer deeper into India: the star-arc is drawn as a nakshatra road, the 27 lunar mansions that were this land’s original fixed points for time and navigation.

The India in it

The nakshatra road

Before compasses, India’s sky was already a calendar and a map. The moon lodges in a different house each night — twenty-seven nakshatras, named in the Vedas, older than the texts that name them. Farmers sowed by them; sailors out of Lothal steered by them; every Indian almanac still runs on them. The arc in this mark is five mansions of that road, with the steering star raised at the meridian. The sail is Meluha — cargo, craft, ambition — threading its mast through the route itself. Below, the work; above, the fixed points it navigates by. For a lab, that is the whole epistemology in one figure: ship what moves, steer by what doesn’t.

The family

Beside the mihir sun

The two marks are built from the same grammar and tell sibling stories: mihir studies the fixed point (the sun, ringed by its orbit); meluha steers by them (the star road, threaded by the sail). One observatory, one harbour.

Motion note: on the landing page the mark loads in narrative order — horizon first, sail rises, then the five mansions light west to east (the moon’s direction through the nakshatras), the steering star’s rays last. On hover, the wake-line drifts one phase. In dark contexts the night-sky card above is the canonical treatment: the road brightens, the horizon stays ink. Why this one wins the “labs” argument: it is the only mark of the twenty whose story is literally a research method — fixed points, navigation, verification against the sky — while staying unmistakably of this land: the route overhead is the oldest continuously-used star system on earth, and it is Indian.
