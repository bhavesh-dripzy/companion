the lab pantheon — ten names, ten marks

Ten names from the same soil, each carrying a figure old enough to have no author. Every mark obeys the family grammar set by mihir and meluha: a quiet geometric symbol on the 120-unit grid, a confident lowercase wordmark, the kiln palette — and one story deep enough to steer a company.

01

dhyan labs — Bindu, the settled mind

Dhyāna is India's most successful export of a method: the Sanskrit word walked to China and became Ch'an, crossed to Japan and became Zen — but it was coined here, in the Upanishads, as a technique: hold one question until it opens. The mark is that technique drawn. Three rings — the mind's noise — settle toward a single fired point: ekāgratā, one-pointed attention. The rings stop short of the ground line, and the gap they leave is the mark's secret: an empty seat, drawn entirely in negative space, waiting for the practitioner. A lab is exactly this — sustained attention as an instrument.

bheem labs — Gada, strength that keeps its word

Bhīma carried the strength of ten thousand elephants and is remembered for something rarer: he never broke a vow. Every terrible promise made at the dice game, he kept — to the letter, years later. His gada is drawn upright, resting, a pillar before it is a weapon. Around the head, a faint kiln-gradient ring traces the arc of the swing he never needs to take — power held in reserve, which is the only kind worth branding. For a lab: raw throughput, blunt honesty, and the discipline that every commitment ships. The mace stands on the ground line like a column of the house it protects.

03

mihir labs — Madhyāhna, the zero-shadow noon

A fourth concept for the sun-name, dug one layer deeper than Udaya, Yantra and Graha. Varahamihira worked at Ujjain — the prime meridian of Indian astronomy, the Greenwich every siddhānta measured longitude from for a thousand years. And the Tropic of Cancer grazes Ujjain: twice a year, at local noon, a gnomon there casts no shadow at all. The dashed arc is the sun's day-path with the indigo suns of ordinary days at its ends; the fired sun sits at the exact apex, dead above the gnomon. Nothing decorates the moment — the proof is pure alignment. For a lab, that is the ambition itself: build the instrument so true that the shadow disappears.

dhanu labs — Lakshya, only the eye

Drona hung a wooden bird in a tree and asked each prince what he saw. Branches, sky, the bird, said the others — and were told to lower their bows. Arjuna said: "only the eye." Dhanu is the bow — Dhanurveda its science, Dhanu rāśi its month in the sky — and the mark holds the bow at full draw, the moment all of archery compresses into: arc, string, arrow, and beyond the tip a small indigo eye, ringed once, waiting. Pure geometry — an arc, its chord bent to the nock, a 45° vector — storing tension the way a lab stores intention: aim long before release, and see nothing but the target.

05

shesh labs — Ananta, what remains

Śeṣa means remainder. In the Purāṇas he is the serpent on whose coils Vishnu sleeps between universes — when each cosmos dissolves, Shesha is what remains to seed the next. And in Indian mathematics śeṣa is the literal remainder of division: Aryabhata's kuṭṭaka algorithm — the pulverizer, fifth century — runs on chasing the śeṣa down to its last term, the same loop inside every modular inverse and every key exchange today. One word, both meanings: the invariant that survives the churn, and the residue that drives the algorithm. Three coils cradle a fired world at rest; the outermost rises to a raised indigo head, watching. Build the substrate that remains.

jatayu labs — Sākshi, the witness wing

An old vulture saw a god-king carrying off a weeping woman, knew precisely how the fight would end, and fought anyway. Jatayu lost his wings to Ravana's blade — and with his last breath delivered the single most valuable data point in the Ramayana: which way south. The first dying log message in literature; the search party owed everything to it. Three feather strokes rise off the ground line — the wing is fallen but the message still climbs — and above the tip a small steering star (the family's navigation motif) extends one ray, indigo and longer than the rest, pointing south. For a lab: observability as devotion. Watch everything, tell the truth, make the last byte count.

kurma labs — Ādhāra, the back beneath the churn

When the gods and asuras churned the ocean for amrita, Mount Mandara began to sink — so Vishnu became a tortoise, dove under, and bore the mountain on his back for a thousand years. Nobody saw him. Everybody drank the nectar. India never forgot the lesson: to this day the foundation stone laid beneath every temple is called the kūrma-śilā — the tortoise stone. The mark is the whole machine in section: ribbed shell below, churn-axis rising from its apex, Vasuki's rope wrapped twice in gradient, and the amrita emerging at the top. Infrastructure as avatar — the platform team of the cosmos. Every churn needs a back to stand on; be the back.

08

croxodile labs — Nakra, the waterline

The crocodilians of India's rivers — the mugger, and the gharial found nowhere else on earth — run on a design that is 200 million years old. It outlasted the dinosaurs by refusing to chase fads: hold at the waterline, spend nothing, show only the eyes. Ninety percent of the animal is below the surface — like all serious work. The mark draws exactly that: one eye bump above the ink waterline, the long patient snout, and the body as a single submerged gradient sweep. At the snout tip, the only warm strokes in the figure: an X — the name's rogue letter made literal, the sub-second snap that all that stillness was for. Wait like a log; strike like a closing jaw.

09

makar labs — Toraṇa, the gateway of the turning sun

The makara is India's emblem of synthesis: a creature assembled from parts — crocodile jaw, elephant trunk, fish tail, peacock plume — that guards every threshold worth crossing. It is Ganga's mount, the device on Kamadeva's flag, the mouth every temple's water exits through, and the spiral-tailed terminal of the makara-toraṇa, the arch over a temple door. And once a year the sun itself crosses into Makara — Sankranti, the turn north toward light, the only Indian festival pinned to the solar calendar. The mark is the doorway with the turning sun inside: arch, jambs, two curled makara tails, an indigo keystone. A lab that builds gateways — and composite creatures from well-chosen parts.

10

ugra labs — Tejas, the fire in the vessel

Ugra — "the fierce" — is the form held in reserve. It is the eighth name of Rudra and the face Vishnu wore exactly once, as Narasimha: the fury engineered to fit through every loophole — neither man nor beast, neither day nor night, neither inside nor out. In tattva symbolism fire is the upward triangle, so the mark holds a kiln flame inside the agni triangle — method containing intensity — and the flame's core is ivory: white heat, hotter than any colour. At the apex the vessel does not quite close; one ink spark rides above the gap. That is the brand's promise and warning in a single dot: gentle by default, and for the problems that yield to nothing else, a fierceness no vessel fully contains.

How the pantheon hangs together: every mark shares the family DNA — one ink ground/horizon line, one kiln-gradient focal point, indigo reserved for the decision in each story (the bird's eye, the south ray, the serpent's head, the keystone, the meridian suns) — so any one of these can stand beside mihir and meluha as a sibling, or the whole set can live as a product family under one house. If choosing one: the strongest pure "labs" stories are kurma (infrastructure as avatar), shesh (the remainder — myth and mathematics in one word) and mihir-madhyāhna (the zero-shadow instrument). The boldest brand bets are croxodile (the X earns its strangeness) and ugra (a name that picks its customers). The warmest are dhyan and jatayu — attention and witness, the two halves of care.
